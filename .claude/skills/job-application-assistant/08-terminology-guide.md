# Terminology Guide - ATS, Screening Software, and Recruiter Red Flags / Green Flags

## Purpose

Brandon's background includes federally regulated defense/firearms manufacturing (ATF Explosive Manufacturing License, destructive-device-class products, ITAR). That background is a genuine asset for defense, industrial, and regulated-manufacturing roles, and a potential distraction or red flag for mainstream corporate/SaaS roles where a screener (human or ATS/recruiting software - Oracle Taleo, Workday, iCIMS, LinkedIn Recruiter, etc.) has no context for it and pattern-matches on scary-sounding nouns.

This is **truthful reframing, not concealment.** Every phrase below describes the same real work in accurate language. Nothing here should ever be used to answer a direct question dishonestly - if an interviewer asks "what did GCDL Defense manufacture," the honest, specific answer is given every time. This guide governs word choice on a document a screener skims in six seconds, not what gets said out loud to a person asking a direct question. Same rule as `03-writing-style.md`'s "interview backtrack test": if a phrase would require Brandon to say "well, what I actually meant was..." in an interview, it's too far and should not be used.

## How to Use This

- **Sales track (mainstream corporate/SaaS roles):** default to the "Screener-Neutral Phrasing" column. The regulated-industry specifics are true but not the point of the application - lead with the transferable sales/ops skill.
- **Founder track, when the target is itself a defense/industrial/regulated-manufacturing employer:** the specific terms are a credibility signal, not a risk - use the "Full Specific Terms" column. Knowing the actual regulatory regime (ATF, ITAR, DCMA) is exactly what that audience wants to see.
- **Founder track, when the target is NOT defense/industrial** (e.g., a general executive/GM role at an unrelated company): still default to Screener-Neutral phrasing for the regulated-industry details, but keep the founder-level scope (capital raised, P&L ownership, headcount) explicit - that part is the selling point regardless of industry.

## Phrasing Bank

| Full Specific Terms (defense/industrial audience) | Screener-Neutral Phrasing (general corporate audience) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ATF Explosive Manufacturing License | Federal manufacturing license / federally licensed manufacturing operation | Keep "federal" - it signals rigor without naming the specific regime |
| Destructive-device-class manufacturer | Highly regulated specialty manufacturer / regulated industrial manufacturer | "Destructive device" is an ATF classification term, not marketing language - almost never helps outside the industry |
| Ammunition manufacturing | Regulated consumer/industrial products manufacturing | Use only when the sales/ops skill is the point, not the product category |
| ITAR compliance | Export-controlled compliance / federal compliance regime | ITAR is recognizable to defense/aerospace recruiters; unfamiliar and vaguely alarming elsewhere |
| DCMA oversight | Government contract oversight / federal contract compliance | |
| Federal Contracting (SAM, CAGE, DFARS) | Federal contracting certifications | Keep the acronyms only when the target company is itself a federal contractor - they're a login-credential-style credibility signal there, noise elsewhere |
| Severe-service industrial valves | Industrial equipment / capital equipment | Use for oil & gas / refining / mining audiences vs. general enterprise |
| Nobel Sport / Browning shotshell distribution | Exclusive long-term distribution agreement with a global manufacturer | The deal mechanics (12-year exclusive, $3M minimum) are the sales story; the product category is incidental outside sporting-goods/firearms audiences |

## General ATS / Screening-Software Green Flags

Words and patterns that score well across most ATS keyword-matching and most human first-pass screens, when truthfully applicable:
- Exact job-title language mirrored from the posting (if the posting says "Enterprise Account Executive," use that phrase somewhere, not just "sales executive")
- Concrete numbers attached to claims: dollar figures, percentages, account counts, timeframes
- Named methodologies or tools the posting itself names (CRM/ERP systems, sales frameworks) - only when genuinely used
- Standard enterprise-sales vocabulary: "pipeline," "quota," "outbound," "consultative selling," "multi-stakeholder," "enterprise accounts," "Fortune 1000/500"
- Action verbs at the start of bullets, varied across the document (see slop checklist in `03-writing-style.md` for the failure mode of repeating the same verb)

## General Red Flags to Avoid (regardless of industry)

- Vague claims with no attached number or artifact ("results-driven," "proven track record" - see `03-writing-style.md` slop checklist)
- Legal/regulatory jargon presented with no plain-language gloss (a term like "DCMA oversight" dropped with zero context reads as noise to a screener who doesn't recognize it)
- Anything that could scan as a job-hopping pattern without explanation - Brandon's history is founder-heavy with overlapping exit timelines (April 2026 for two ventures); when the CV's date ranges alone might read as churn, the profile statement should carry one clause of context ("recently exited two ventures, transitioning to full-time enterprise sales") rather than leaving the reader to guess. This is already standard practice in the current CV/cover letter drafts - keep it there for every sales-track application.

## What NOT to Do

- Do not remove a regulated-industry credential or bullet entirely just because it might raise an eyebrow - omission of a real job is a bigger red flag in a background check than accurate, neutral phrasing of it. Reframe, do not delete, unless `05-cv-templates.md`'s relevance-weighted cutting rules call for cutting it for space (a different reason than image management).
- Do not invent a softer job title or company description that isn't true (e.g., do not describe American Munitions as a generic "consumer goods manufacturer" - "regulated manufacturer" is accurate, "consumer goods" is not, since the product itself is federally controlled).
- Do not apply Screener-Neutral phrasing inside anything that will be independently verified against public records (e.g., don't undersell a defense-manufacturing role to a defense-industry employer who will recognize the actual company and product line immediately - see "How to Use This" above).
